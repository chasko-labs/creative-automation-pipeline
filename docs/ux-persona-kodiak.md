# Who runs this pipeline at Kodiak — built for marketing teams, not engineers

This page is for the people who will actually open the brief, pick a message, and launch ads. No short forms, no code words. Every step is written so a brand manager, a field marketer, or a brand ambassador can do it the first time without asking engineering for help.

## Primary person: Maya, Kodiak Brand Manager in Park City

**Who she is**
- 32, based at headquarters among the Wasatch Mountains, manages the flapjack and waffle mix line plus Bear Bites and the oatmeal cups. She owns the voice that says "real food for real adventures."
- Her week is split between three places: the test kitchen, the photo library, and the retail calendar. She knows the red wagon story by heart — Joel at eight years old, a wagon of paper bags, the whole street — and she protects the bear.
- She works with sales leads for three very different store groups: the big membership stores where families buy in bulk, the style-forward national chain where younger shoppers look for clean labels, and the friendly southern supermarkets where Sunday breakfast is family time.

**What her day looks like when she launches a campaign**
- She starts with a one-page idea sheet. Not a ticket, just a short form: which places she wants to reach, who she wants to talk to, and the single line she wants people to remember.
- She picks a photo if she has one — a real stack, a real cabin, a real kid with a Bear Bite — or she lets the system make a new photo that matches the Kodiak look. She never has to resize that photo herself.
- She sees three finished ads for every product — one square, one tall, one wide — each with the bear in the corner, the warm orange bar at the bottom, and her line centered over a soft dark band so it reads. She checks the little green badges that say the bear is present and the colors are right. If something is flagged, it tells her in plain words what to fix, not an error code.
- She chooses where each ad goes: the membership stores in the west, the national chain in the Midwest, the southern supermarkets, the small town grocers around Las Cruces, the diner down the road, or the direct home delivery subscription. One click sends the right ad to the right place.

**What frustrates her today that this pipeline removes**
- Doing the same resize and rewording 30 times by hand.
- Waiting two weeks for an agency to move a logo 20 pixels.
- Launching a great photo that never gets used because the southern stores needed green chile and the mountain stores needed Wasatch snow, and there was no time to make both.

**How we measure if Maya wins**
- She can go from idea sheet to ready-to-launch ads in ten minutes, not ten days.
- Every ad looks like Kodiak even when it was made in Las Cruces or for a small diner in Alamogordo.
- She can see, per town and per store group, which photo and which line got someone to find the store.

## Second person: Diego, Field Brand Ambassador for the Southwest

**Who he is**
- 26, lives in Albuquerque, covers New Mexico and west Texas. He knows which store manager in Las Cruces puts the flapjack mix next to the green chile, which diner in Alamogordo flips Kodiak cakes on Saturday morning, and which Target on Route 70 runs out by Thursday.
- He carries samples and a phone. His training came from the Kodiak ambassador guide — the video, the two-page brand vibe sheet, and the feedback form that asks how many people he served and how many samples he handed out.

**What he needs**
- A social post he can share that feels like his corner, not headquarters. "Green chile flapjacks in Las Cruces — find us at your Walmart and Albertsons" with a photo that shows green chile and a cast iron skillet, not a generic stack.
- A way to tell Maya what worked — "green chile got twice the saves in Las Cruces vs plain buttermilk" — without writing a report.

**How the pipeline helps him**
- He picks "Las Cruces" and "Green Chile" from a simple menu of places and flavors. The ad arrives with the right town name already in it and a map button that points to the nearest store.
- His samples and his social post are linked — the same code that tracks his handing out samples also tracks whether the ad in his zip code drove someone to get directions.

## Third person: Priya, Performance Marketing Lead

**Who she is**
- 38, runs the paid social for all retail partners from one central place. She doesn't want to make 30 budgets by hand. She wants one post that intelligently shows the right town name and the right store map to whoever is looking.

**What she needs**
- A single master post per product, not 30 copies. The system should automatically show "Find us today in {{your town}}!" and the right store map to a person in Chicago versus a person in Miami, and a button that says "Get Directions" that opens the nearest store that actually carries Kodiak.

**How the pipeline helps her**
- She uploads the full store list once. The pipeline organizes stores into groups (by retailer, by city cluster, by small independents). She sets one budget per city group, but keeps one creative. The platform handles showing the right local text and the right map card. Her report comes back as one line per creative per region — not a spreadsheet she has to stitch by hand.

## How the three work together in one week

Monday, Maya writes: "Target region New Mexico, city Las Cruces, audience families 28-45, message Green Chile Frontier — try Bear Bites with roasted Hatch." Diego sees that message pop up for his territory and shares the 9 by 16 story to his local audience. Priya watches the dashboard — Las Cruces Target shows higher direction taps than the control. Friday, that learning is saved as training data: region = New Mexico, market = Las Cruces, audience = green chile families, message = green chile frontier. Next time someone picks Las Cruces, the system suggests that message first because it worked there.

No one had to learn a new acronym. The bear stayed the bear. The store list stayed the store list. The photo library stayed the photo library.
